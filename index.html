Abstract

This page summarises how the universe expands, beginning with Hubble's discovery that galaxies are moving away from us when their light is redshifted. It shows how the scale factor and Friedmann's equations describe the growth of the Universe and illustrate its different possible shapes. It also highlights how matter density and dark energy can change the Universe's evolution and its potential long-term fate.

Introduction

Our understanding of the universe has transformed dramatically since the early twentieth century. Einstein's theory provided a new framework for describing gravity. Edwin Hubble's observations then revealed the true scale of the cosmos. Galaxies beyond the Milky Way were moving away from us, as shown by their redshift, indicating that the universe is expanding. Hubble's law forms one of the foundations of modern cosmology. Building on these ideas, scientists use mathematical tools such as Friedmann's equations and the scale factor to illustrate how the universe expands and how its geometry may be flat, open or closed, depending on its density. These ideas allow us to explore how the universe has evolved and what its ultimate fate may be.

Analysis and discussion

Hubble's Law and Redshift: Math of the Expanding Universe

According to The Physics of the Universe (n.d.) , Albert Einstein developed his theory of gravity in the early twentieth century, at a time when astronomers believed the Milky Way was the only galaxy. He assumed that, on a large scale, the universe has the same properties everywhere and appears identical in every direction. In 1925, Edwin Hubble transformed this view by demonstrating that many galaxies exist beyond the Milky Way. Four years later, Hubble observed that light from distant galaxies was shifted towards the red end of the spectrum because of the Doppler effect. This phenomenon, known as redshift, occurs when light from an object moving away from the observer is stretched to longer wavelengths. By studying the redshifts of Cepheid variable stars, Hubble concluded that galaxies were moving away from one another, providing strong evidence that the universe is expanding. His observations also showed that the farther a galaxy is from Earth, the faster it appears to recede. The StarChild Team (n.d.) explains that Hubble's Law is written as:

v = H 0 D

Here, v represents the recession velocity, D is the distance to the galaxy, and H0 is the Hubble constant. Although the exact value of the Hubble constant remains uncertain, it is commonly estimated to be around 65 km/s/Mpc (1 Mpc = 3 × 106 parsecs, with one parsec being approximately 3.26 light-years). This relationship supports the conclusion that the universe is continually expanding.

The Scale Factor: Measuring How the Universe Grows

Peacock (1999) explains that the equation describing the scale factor can be derived using a quasi-Newtonian approach. By considering a sphere with radius R, where r is an arbitrary distance, the motion of material at the sphere's edge is determined only by the mass contained within it. This leads directly to Friedmann's equation, which represents the conservation of energy:

r ˙ 2 − G M R r = constant

Friedmann's equation predicts different outcomes depending on the curvature of the universe. A closed universe (k = +1) has negative total energy, meaning gravity will eventually overcome the expansion and cause the universe to collapse again. By contrast, an open universe (k = −1) has enough energy to continue expanding forever. Peacock compares this behaviour to a cannonball fired vertically from Earth: if its speed exceeds the escape velocity, it will never fall back. In the same way, a universe expanding fast enough will continue expanding indefinitely.

Flat, Open, or Closed? The Geometry of the Universe

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (2000) explains that the terms flat, open, and closed describe both the density of the universe and the curvature of space-time. A universe with a very high density would eventually stop expanding and collapse under its own gravity, while one with a low density would continue expanding forever. A flat universe has a critical density that allows expansion to continue without either collapsing or slowing to a complete stop.

The curvature of space-time describes how matter and energy affect the shape of the universe. Since this is difficult to imagine in three dimensions, it is often explained using two-dimensional examples. A positively curved universe resembles the surface of a sphere, whereas a negatively curved universe is similar to a saddle. A flat universe, by comparison, can be represented by a sheet of paper where parallel lines remain parallel. Current observations strongly suggest that the universe is flat. Inflation theory proposes that the universe experienced a brief period of extremely rapid expansion shortly after the Big Bang, helping to explain its large-scale uniformity. If the universe is indeed flat while containing relatively little ordinary matter, an additional form of energy is required. This is known as the cosmological constant, or dark energy, which is believed to drive the accelerating expansion of the universe. If dark energy remains constant over time, the expansion of the universe will continue to accelerate indefinitely.

The Fate of the Universe: Math Predicts the End

NASA Goddard Space Flight Center (n.d.) explains that there are two main possibilities for the ultimate fate of the universe: eternal expansion or the Big Crunch. Which outcome occurs depends on the balance between the expansion of the universe and the gravitational attraction produced by matter. The Hubble constant measures the current rate of expansion, while gravity depends on the density and pressure of matter throughout the universe. If the average density remains below the critical value, gravity will never be strong enough to stop the expansion. Instead, the universe will continue expanding and gradually cool over time in a scenario known as the Big Freeze or Big Chill, eventually becoming too cold to support life. However, if the density exceeds the critical value, gravity will eventually overcome the expansion, causing the universe to contract until it collapses in an event known as the Big Crunch.
