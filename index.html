Abstract

This page summarises how the universe expands; to start with, Hubble's discovery that galaxies are moving away from us when their light is redshifted. This shows how the scale factor and Friedmann's equations display the growth of the Universe and illustrate the different possible shapes of the universe. It also highlights how matter density and dark energy can change the universe's evolution and its potential long-term fate.

Introduction

Our understanding of the universe has transformed dramatically since the early twentieth century. Einstein's theory provides a new framework for describing gravity. On the other hand, Edwin Hubble's observations revealed the true scale of the cosmos. Galaxies beyond the Milky Way were moving away from us due to redshift, indicating that the universe is expanding. Hubble's law forms the foundations of modern cosmology. Building on these ideas, scientists utilise mathematical tools such as Friedmann's equations and the scale factor to illustrate how the universe expands and how its geometry is either flat, open or closed, depending on its density. These ideas allow us to explore how the universe has evolved and what its ultimate fate may be.

Analysis and discussion

Hubble's Law and Redshift: Mathematics of the Expanding Universe

The Physics of the Universe (no date) describes that Albert Einstein had formulated his theory of gravity in the early twentieth century, when astronomers knew only of our own galaxy. Einstein assumed the universe has the same broad properties everywhere and that it looks the same in every direction, regardless of where an observer is. In 1925, the American astronomer Edwin Hubble shocked the scientific community by showing that the universe was more than just the Milky Way galaxy. Later, in 1929, Hubble discovered that the light from these other galaxies was slightly shifted towards the red end of the spectrum due to the Doppler effect. This shift is called redshift. Redshift occurs when the wavelength of light increases as the source of light moves away from the observer. The Doppler shift is the change in the wavelength of observed light due to relative motion between the wave source and the observer. After analysing the redshifts of galaxies and using observations of Cepheid variable stars, Hubble concluded that galaxies and clusters of galaxies were moving apart and therefore the universe was expanding. Hubble's law shows that in an expanding universe, a galaxy's recession velocity is directly proportional to its distance. The StarChild Team (no date) states that Hubble's law is usually expressed as:

v = H 0 D

In this equation, v is the recession velocity, D is the distance to the galaxy and H0 is the Hubble constant that relates them. The source used in this article gives an approximate value of 65 km/s/Mpc. One megaparsec is approximately three million parsecs, while one parsec is approximately 3.26 light-years. Using this value, a galaxy one megaparsec away would be moving away at approximately 65 kilometres per second.

The Scale Factor: Measuring How the Universe Grows

Peacock (1999) explains that the equation for the scale factor's motion can be derived in a quasi-Newtonian manner. If we consider a sphere centred on an arbitrary point, the motion of a point at the edge of the sphere will be influenced by the mass contained inside it. A differential equation can then be written to express conservation of energy. This leads to a form of Friedmann's equation, which describes the relationship between expansion, gravity, matter and the geometry of the universe. The Friedmann equation shows that a universe that is spatially closed, with k = +1, has negative total energy. In a simple matter-dominated model, the expansion could eventually halt because of gravity and the universe could re-collapse. An unbound model is spatially open, with k = -1, and can continue expanding. The behaviour can be compared to a cannonball fired vertically against Earth's gravity. Earth's gravity defines an escape velocity for projectiles, so an object travelling above escape velocity continues moving away.

Flat, Open or Closed? The Geometry of the Universe

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (2000) suggests that when astronomers describe the universe as flat, open or closed, they are referring to the shape or curvature of space-time and its relationship with the universe's density. A universe with positive curvature can be described as closed. In two dimensions, positive curvature can be compared to the surface of a sphere. An open universe has negative curvature, which can be compared to a saddle-shaped surface on which parallel lines diverge. A flat two-dimensional universe can be compared to a sheet of paper on which parallel lines remain parallel. Many independent observations indicate that the universe is close to spatially flat. Inflation theory states that a small portion of the early universe underwent extremely rapid expansion shortly after the Big Bang. This helps to explain the smoothness and homogeneity of regions of space that would otherwise never have been in contact. If the universe is flat and the density of matter is too low to provide the required total density, another component is needed. This component is commonly associated with the cosmological constant or another form of dark energy. Dark energy can cause the expansion of the universe to accelerate.

The Fate of the Universe: Mathematics Predicts the End

NASA Goddard Space Flight Center (no date) explains that possible futures for the universe include endless expansion and collapse. The evolution of the universe depends on the relationship between the momentum of expansion and the gravitational effects of its matter and energy. The current expansion rate is connected to the Hubble constant, while gravitational behaviour depends on the density and pressure of the universe's contents. If the density is below the critical density in a simple model, there may not be enough gravitational attraction to stop the expansion. The universe could continue expanding and cooling, leading to a possible future known as the Big Freeze or Big Chill. If the density is greater than the critical density and no other component prevents collapse, gravity could eventually stop the expansion and reverse it. This possible outcome is called the Big Crunch. Modern cosmological models must also consider dark energy, because accelerating expansion changes the relationship between the universe's geometry and its final fate.
