Abstract

This page summarises how the universe expands, beginning with Hubble's discovery that galaxies are moving away from us when their light is redshifted. It shows how the scale factor and Friedmann's equations describe the growth of the Universe and illustrate its different possible shapes. It also highlights how matter density and dark energy can change the Universe's evolution and its potential long-term fate.

Introduction

Our understanding of the universe has transformed dramatically since the early twentieth century. Einstein's theory provided a new framework for describing gravity. Edwin Hubble's observations then revealed the true scale of the cosmos. Galaxies beyond the Milky Way were moving away from us, as shown by their redshift, indicating that the universe is expanding. Hubble's law forms one of the foundations of modern cosmology. Building on these ideas, scientists use mathematical tools such as Friedmann's equations and the scale factor to illustrate how the universe expands and how its geometry may be flat, open or closed, depending on its density. These ideas allow us to explore how the universe has evolved and what its ultimate fate may be.

Analysis and discussion

Hubble's Law and Redshift: Mathematics of the Expanding Universe

The Physics of the Universe (no date) describes how Albert Einstein formulated his theory of gravity in the early twentieth century, when astronomers knew only of our own galaxy. Einstein assumed that the universe has the same broad properties everywhere and looks the same in every direction, regardless of the observer's position. In 1925, the American astronomer Edwin Hubble changed the scientific view of the cosmos by showing that the universe contained more than the Milky Way. Later, in 1929, Hubble discovered that light from other galaxies was shifted towards the red end of the spectrum. This effect is called redshift and occurs when the wavelength of light increases as its source moves away from the observer. The Doppler shift is the change in the observed wavelength caused by relative motion between a wave source and an observer. After analysing the redshifts of galaxies and using observations of Cepheid variable stars, Hubble concluded that galaxies and clusters of galaxies were moving apart and that the universe was expanding. The StarChild Team (no date) states that Hubble's law is usually expressed as:

v = H 0 D

Here, v is the recession velocity, D is the distance to the galaxy and H0 is the Hubble constant. The source used in this article gives an approximate value of 65 km/s/Mpc. One megaparsec is approximately three million parsecs, and one parsec is approximately 3.26 light-years. This means that, using that value, a galaxy one megaparsec away would be moving away at approximately 65 kilometres per second.

The Scale Factor: Measuring How the Universe Grows

Peacock (1999) explains that the equation for the scale factor's motion can be derived in a quasi-Newtonian manner. Consider a sphere centred on an arbitrary point. The motion of a point at the edge of the sphere is influenced by the mass inside it. A differential equation can then be written to express conservation of energy. This leads to a form of the Friedmann equation, which describes the relationship between expansion, gravity, matter and the geometry of the universe. The Friedmann equation shows that a spatially closed universe, with k = +1, has negative total energy. In a simple matter-dominated model, its expansion could eventually halt and reverse. A spatially open universe, with k = -1, is unbound and can continue expanding. The behaviour can be compared to a projectile fired vertically against Earth's gravity. A projectile below escape velocity returns, whereas one above escape velocity continues travelling away.

Flat, Open or Closed? The Geometry of the Universe

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (2000) explains that when astronomers describe the universe as flat, open or closed, they are referring to the curvature of space-time and its relationship with the density of the universe. A closed universe has positive curvature and can be compared in two dimensions to the surface of a sphere. An open universe has negative curvature and can be compared to a saddle, on which parallel lines diverge. A flat universe can be compared to a sheet of paper, on which parallel lines remain parallel. Many observations indicate that the universe is close to spatially flat. Inflation theory proposes that a small region of the early universe underwent extremely rapid expansion shortly after the Big Bang. This helps to explain the large-scale smoothness and homogeneity of regions that would otherwise not have been in causal contact. If the universe is flat but its matter density is too low to provide the required total density, another component is needed. This component is commonly associated with dark energy or the cosmological constant. Dark energy can cause the expansion of the universe to accelerate.

The Fate of the Universe: Mathematics Predicts the End

NASA Goddard Space Flight Center (no date) explains that possible futures for the universe include endless expansion and collapse. The evolution of the universe depends on the relationship between its expansion and the gravitational effects of its matter and energy. The expansion rate is connected to the Hubble constant, while gravitational behaviour depends on the density and pressure of the universe's contents. If the density is below the critical density in a simple model, there may not be enough gravitational attraction to stop the expansion. The universe could continue expanding and cooling, leading to a future commonly called the Big Freeze or Big Chill. If the density is greater than the critical density and no other component prevents collapse, gravity could eventually stop the expansion and reverse it. This possible outcome is called the Big Crunch. Modern cosmological models must also account for dark energy, because an accelerating expansion changes the connection between geometry and the universe's final fate.
